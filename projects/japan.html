Towards the Japanese Agentic State

Download the Japanese vision paper Download the glossary

24.08.2026

Since the publication of the vision paper on the Agentic State, governments across the world have shown interest in exploring what the vision could mean in their respective contexts.

Today, we are publishing the first official translation of the vision paper, making the full document accessible to Japanese-speaking readers (download). This initiative is part of a broader effort to make the ideas in the vision paper accessible to non-English-speaking audiences.

The translation process brought us to examine more closely the meaning of some of the concepts at the heart of the paper — including the distinction between agentic and autonomous, and terms such as agent governance, public value, and human-AI collaboration. For this reason, alongside the translated vision paper, we are publishing a companion glossary that aligns core English concepts with Japanese terminology and short explanations. The aim is to allow the discussion to travel with greater precision across policy, technology, and public administration communities.

The translation follows growing interest in Japan. Takashi Asanuma, Senior Advisor to the Minister for Digital Transformation and former Vice-Minister for Digital Transformation at the Digital Agency of Japan, published an in-depth analysis of the vision paper and Ukraine’s roadmap through the lens of Japanese public administration. You can also read Asanuma’s essay on X: “エージェント型行政（Agentic State）の現在地”.

His analysis explores how the twelve layers map onto Japan’s existing foundations, from the My Number system to base registries and the government cloud. It discusses why the shift is not simply about adding AI to government, but about rebuilding public administration around systems that can understand situations and act, while humans retain oversight, judgement, and accountability.

The Agentic State is a global idea, but it will only become useful if it can be debated in the institutional language of each country. Japan brings its own administrative traditions, digital foundations, and reform questions to the agenda. A shared vocabulary is a first step toward making that debate practical.

From Tallinn to Tokyo, the question is the same: how should government work when AI agents can act?
